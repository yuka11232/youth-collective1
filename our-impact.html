Engineering workshops · Mentorship

People, Patterns & Prototypes.

Our engineering workshops start somewhere unexpected: with people, not code or circuit diagrams. Students watch how others use everyday objects, notice where they get stuck, and work out why some designs just work better. Then they open up real prototypes, and finally build their own.

Inside our workshops

Learning engineering by taking it apart.

The approach behind the workshops

Youssef El Haroun · Lead co-founder

Youssef developed the workshop concept and structured how each session runs. His central idea is to teach engineering through reverse engineering: instead of starting with theory, he brings real prototypes into the classroom, shows how they work, and asks students to examine the parts, question the design choices and say what they would change.

He organizes the workshops, coordinates the mentors, and guides student teams through the engineering process, from a first idea toward a working prototype.

How a workshop unfolds

Observe→Understand→Build

  1. 01 · Observe

    Observe like an anthropologist

    Students examine everyday problems, human habits, and how people actually interact with objects and technology. They bring their own questions, observations and ideas, and those become the starting point for everything that follows.

    The question we ask “Why do people use this object this way, and what could make it easier?”

  2. 02 · Understand

    Think like a reverse engineer

    We demonstrate existing prototypes and open them up: sensors, circuits, materials and software. Students handle the components themselves, discuss why each design choice was made, and suggest how they would improve it.

    The question we ask “What does this part do, and why is it here instead of something else?”

  3. 03 · Build

    Build like an inventor

    Students put what they have seen into practice, at a level that fits their age.

    Younger students Take part in guided engineering activities, building and testing with a mentor close by.
    Teenagers Work in teams on their own prototypes: making the technical decisions, testing ideas and improving their designs with mentorship.

Recognizing student contributions

Built by students, supported by mentors.

Students in these workshops are not an audience. They ask the questions, make the decisions and do the building. Mentors explain, ask questions back, and step in when a team is stuck.

Younger participants

Curiosity, then understanding

They develop curiosity and a practical feel for how things work by handling real components, wiring their own circuits and asking why.

Teenage teams

Ownership of their own projects

They take ownership of their engineering projects: choosing the problem, deciding on the design, building the prototype and improving it after every test.

  • Generating ideas

  • Choosing components
  • Making technical decisions

Where it led

Three student-team prototypes. 452 people served.

Through this approach, three student teams developed prototypes with mentorship, and those prototypes have together served 452 people.

The psychology and anthropology here are a way of thinking, not formal research. We ask students to watch how people really behave and to take those habits seriously when they design.

Try it yourself

Five-minute observation challenge

  1. Pick something you use every day: a door handle, a backpack, a water bottle.
  2. Watch someone else use it. Where do they slow down or hesitate?
  3. Ask them why. Then sketch one change that would make it easier.

That’s step one of every workshop.

Project · MathRoutes

Every child has their own route through math.

MathRoutes is a free, non-profit math program for children aged 7–9 with ADHD. Instead of long columns of sums, children learn through interactive paper cards they color, draw on, cut out and arrange, often without noticing they are doing math at all.

Created by Ilyas Taghiyev, Youth Collective’s Youth Data Science Educator & Children’s Learning Ambassador. Sessions are led by trained school-age mentors, with a teacher present at every meeting.

A small hurricane, given somewhere to go.

A child with ADHD can feel like a small hurricane: a hundred thoughts at once, hands that want to hold something, eyes looking for something new. In a traditional lesson that energy has nowhere to go. The teacher explains once, the moment passes, and the gap between the child and math grows a little every day.

Ilyas started from a simple question: instead of fitting the child to the math, what if we fit the math to the child? As he puts it, these children don’t struggle because they lack energy. They struggle because their energy has no room.

Each card is short: one task, one step, a few minutes. A long road feels like a mountain; short steps feel like a staircase.

  • Color it
    Look closely

    Pictures where the numbers are hidden in the drawing.

  • Connect it
    Find the path

    Routes to join up, step by step.

  • Complete it
    Spot the pattern

    Sequences and shapes waiting to be finished.

  • Build it
    Use your hands

    Towers and models to cut out and assemble.

  • Solve it
    Finish the story

    Small stories with a math question inside.

The same rhythm every session.

For a child with ADHD, a clear routine feels safe. Every meeting moves through the same steps, and help fades until the child solves a card on their own.

  1. 1Warm up
  2. 2Watch once
  3. 3Less and less help
  4. 4All by myself
  5. 5“What helped you today?”

Why school-age mentors

An explorer, not a lecturer.

Children listen more easily to someone close to their own age. A MathRoutes mentor works like an older brother or sister and an explorer at once, watching for what helps each child most: coloring, building, or moving. Every child has their own key, and the mentor’s job is to find it.

  • Done · Sessions with childrenSmall-group sessions with mentors and a teacher present.
  • Now · Improving the cardsReworking them from children’s, mentors’ and teachers’ feedback.
  • Next · Free for everyoneAll cards and a mentor’s guide, published free for teachers and parents.

Project · Olympic Inclusion Lab

Sport where every young person gets a place on the team.

The Olympic Inclusion Lab is Azerbaijan’s National Inclusive Sports Innovation Initiative. Youth Collective contributed to its development: a model where children with intellectual and developmental disabilities and neurotypical young athletes participate, communicate, learn, and grow together through sport — and where young people themselves serve as leaders, Inclusion Ambassadors, peer mentors, researchers, and advocates.

Coordinated for Youth Collective by Aydan Nuriyeva

Project · ReHand Loom

Supporting the hands that preserve Azerbaijan’s carpet traditions.

A carpet takes months, and the weaver’s hands carry every one of them — the same grip, the same knot, repeated until the pattern is finished. ReHand Loom pairs a wearable device with a digital platform to study hand and wrist workload at the loom, and shares that information with specialists who give each artisan individualized advice on working practices and load distribution.

Created and presented by Mikayil Ahmadbayli, Youssef El Haroun and Ilyas Taghiyev — equal collaborators at every stage, from build to presentation.

A wearable, and a platform to make sense of it.

The wearable is deliberately low-cost — off-the-shelf sensors, a small controller, and a housing the team assembled themselves — so it can be reproduced rather than locked behind a price tag. It records how the hand and wrist are actually loaded during a working session.

The digital platform turns those readings into a report a specialist can review, and the specialist responds with advice tailored to that artisan’s own way of working. The device measures and reports; the specialist advises. It is a support tool and does not replace professional care.

  • Flex
    Finger bend

    Tracks how far each finger curls through the knotting motion.

  • Pressure
    Grip force

    Measures how hard the hand is gripping, and for how long.

  • Motion
    Hand movement

    Captures the repetition and rhythm of the weaving gesture.

Reach so far

Outreach, support and training outcomes reported by the project team. They describe participation, specialist review and training activity — not clinically proven recovery.

378 weavers and traditional artisans reached
124 participants individually supported through reports reviewed by 7 specialists
53 cultural staff and local coordinators trained across 5 Azerbaijani regions
8 independently delivered sessions across 3 regions
6 feedback-driven design iterations

Carried on locally

The sessions kept running after the team went home.

Eight of the sessions were delivered by trained local partners on their own. Cultural staff and regional coordinators ran them using the team’s protocol, training materials and digital reporting template — the same method, in the hands of the people already working closest to the weavers.
